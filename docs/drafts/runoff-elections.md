# Runoff Elections

A runoff is a second election between the top two candidates when no one won enough votes the first time. Most states with
runoffs require a majority, meaning more than half the votes. If no one gets there, the top two meet again a few weeks later,
and only they are on the ballot.

## Key Facts

- [10] states hold runoffs in some party primaries. Most are in the South.
- [3] states can also hold a runoff after the November general election: Georgia, Louisiana and Mississippi.
- The usual rule is a majority. North Carolina and South Dakota use lower bars: more than 30% and 35%.
- The runoff comes two to twelve weeks after the first election, depending on the state.

## How a Runoff Works

Every runoff starts with the same question: did anyone reach the share of votes the state requires?

[Step flow, illustrative]

1. **The first election.** Several candidates run for the same office.
2. **The count.** Officials check whether the leader passed the state's threshold.
3. **Yes:** the leader wins. There's no runoff.
4. **No:** the top two candidates go to a runoff. Everyone else is out.
5. **The runoff.** Whoever gets more votes wins.

Here's an example with made-up numbers. Four candidates split 100 votes 40, 30, 20 and 10. In a majority state, 40 isn't more
than half, so the top two, with 40 and 30, meet in a runoff. In North Carolina, 40 is more than 30%, so the leader would
win outright.

## Primary Runoffs and General Runoffs

Most runoffs happen in party primaries, where a party picks its nominee. A few states also hold them after the November election.

| | Primary runoff | General election runoff |
|---|---|---|
| What it decides | A party's nominee | Who takes office |
| Who's on the ballot | The top two from one party's primary | The top two from the November ballot |
| Where | [10] states, mostly in the South | Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi (statewide offices) |

## Runoffs Compared With Similar Systems

Some systems that sound alike work differently. This table explains how each one works; it doesn't rank them.

| System | How the winner is decided | Second election? |
|---|---|---|
| Runoff | If no one passes the threshold, the top two face off later | Yes, weeks later |
| Top-two primary | The top two in the primary advance to November, whatever their vote share | No separate runoff |
| Ranked choice voting | Voters rank candidates; counting happens in rounds on the same ballots | No |

Alabama uses both for some voters. Military and overseas voters get a ranked ballot in federal primaries with three or more
candidates, other than for President.

## Who Can Vote in a Runoff

For a primary runoff, two things matter: which party's primary you voted in, and whether you voted at all.

**If you voted in a party's primary,** you stay with that party for the runoff. Every state we checked keeps you from crossing
over to another party's runoff. [Count and list from data.]

**If you skipped the first round,** you can still vote in a runoff in [Alabama, Georgia, Oklahoma, South Carolina and Texas],
and you can choose either party's runoff in [Alabama, Georgia, South Carolina and Texas]. [Arkansas, Mississippi, North
Carolina and South Dakota: not yet confirmed on an official page.]

**Registering between rounds** depends on the state:
- **North Carolina:** the runoff continues the first primary, so new registrations aren't accepted in between.
- **Texas:** a new registration takes effect 30 days after it's submitted, so it may not be active in time.
- **South Dakota:** you can register up to 15 days before the runoff.
- **Georgia:** the registration deadline is the same as the first primary's, except for federal races.

## Questions People Ask

**When does a runoff election happen?**
Between two and twelve weeks after the first election. South Carolina's comes two weeks later. Texas holds its primary runoff on
the fourth Tuesday in May, about 12 weeks after the March primary. Your state's page has this year's date.

**What causes a runoff election?**
No candidate reaching the state's threshold, usually because three or more strong candidates split the vote.

**Can I vote in a runoff if I didn't vote the first time?**
In the states we've confirmed so far, yes, if you're registered: [Alabama, Georgia, Oklahoma, South Carolina and Texas]. If
you did vote, you stay with the party whose primary you chose. See Who Can Vote in a Runoff.

**Is a runoff the same as a recount?**
No. A recount counts the same ballots again. A runoff is a new election with a new ballot.

## What This Guide Doesn't Cover

- Runoffs for city, county and school board races, which follow local rules
- Special elections, which can have their own runoff rules
- Arguments for and against runoffs

For your election, your state or local election office has the final word.
