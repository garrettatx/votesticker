## Answer (as returned)

The strongest earlier lead I found is a 1926 Texas League of Women Voters campaign using "I have voted" tags at
election booths, but I could verify it only through a later transcription, not the original publication. It is a
worthwhile lead, not yet a defensible "earliest documented" finding.

**1926: Texas voting tags.** Exact sentence recovered: "'I have voted' tags given out at election booths
stimulated last minute voting." Source actually consulted: a November 2022 AskHistorians answer by jbdyer,
reproducing a passage about a Texas League of Women Voters campaign directed by its president, Mary E. Jagoe. The
surrounding passage describes ballot-marking classes, posters, slogans in public places, and efforts concerning
two primary elections. Evidence status: secondary transcription only; no original periodical issue and page.
Even the quoted sentence establishes tags distributed at election booths, not explicitly after a voter had cast a
ballot. The answer also asserts such items were associated mainly with the League before World War II; not
established.

**1920: A commemorative antecedent.** "Under the 19th Amendment I Cast My First Vote, Nov. 2nd, 1920." Source:
Wikipedia's "I Voted sticker" article describing an NMAH button distributed by the Republican Party in Lancaster,
Pennsylvania. Antecedent, not an answer.

**Undated: A California badge.** Calisphere record titled "I voted! Did You? Badge". Date, maker and circumstances
not verified; the nearby "1900-1950" wording belongs to an unrelated item.

**Miami and competing claims.** Miami 1950: a later article dates the Herald mention to Oct 27, 1950 (still
secondary). Fort Lauderdale 1982: a 2018 Arizona Republic account cites an Oct 29, 1982 Herald article. Phoenix
1985: Vox: "The Phoenix Association of Realtors claims that it invented the 'I Voted Today' sticker in 1985," a
reported claim. Boudreau 1987: histories distinguish her flag design from existing checkmark stickers; she is not
the inventor of the practice.

**What can safely be stated:** a later transcription describes "I have voted" tags at Texas election booths in
1926 during a League of Women Voters campaign; original and timing unverified. No earliest qualifying example
from a primary source was established in this pass.

## Our Follow-Up (primary sources read by us)

Perplexity's lead pointed at "I have voted" tags rather than stickers. A HathiTrust full-text phrase search for
`I have voted tags` (Chrome; 32 results, 12 full view) found three primary sources none of the ranking pages cite.
Snippets read in HathiTrust's in-item search; page images are blocked to automation.

1. **St. Louis Board of Education, Annual Report v. 67 (1920-21), p. 52** (HathiTrust uc1.b2987052, seq 60;
   https://hdl.handle.net/2027/uc1.b2987052?urlappend=%3Bseq=60). A student election at Blewett, a St. Louis
   public school, run with real election forms (the city Board of Election Commissioners qualified the 60 student
   judges and clerks). "All Blewett citizens under 21 years of age who had attended Blewett for at least six days
   on or before election day, October 29, were qualified voters." "the Printing Department printed enough "I have
   voted" tags to give each student one as he voted". "Out of 1250 who registered 1170 or 94% voted". Year 1920
   from the report year and the Junior Life's Oct 28 election issue. **Verified; shipped** as the earliest example
   we found (a tag, a student election).
2. **Book of the Camp Fire Girls (1962), p. 225** (uc1.31175021370898, seq 235): "Give out "I Have Voted" tags on
   election day." **Verified; shipped** (timeline).
3. **U.S. Commission on Civil Rights, Political Participation (1968), p. 113** (uiug.30112105087479, seq 123):
   Americus, Georgia, November 15, 1966 municipal primary: "When the election was held, all clerks and managers at
   the polling place were white, although Negroes were employed to pin "I have voted" tags on the voters as they
   left the polling place." **Verified; shipped** (timeline, with "Black residents" in our words).

Other HathiTrust hits, search-only (no snippet visible, not used): League of Women Voters "Choosing the
President" (1964, 1976, 1984, 1992), Proceedings of an annual state conference 1952/1953, "Guide to community
action" (1954), a DAR Georgia chapters proceedings (1954), "The ORSANCO story" (1967).
