## Method

The source of record for each row is a state statute on an official legislature site, an enrolled act or bill text published by the legislature, a state attorney general opinion, or a page or document from the state election office. **How the Texas sections apply to runoffs.** Voting in a party's primary affiliates you with that party (162.003). Once
affiliated, you cannot affiliate with another party that voting year (162.012). A runoff is a "primary election" because it
is held under Chapter 172 (1.005(14); 172.004), so a vote in another party's runoff is void under 162.013 and knowingly
casting one is an offense under 162.014. Someone who voted in no primary has no affiliation yet, so they can pick either
runoff; voting in it affiliates them. The SOS advisory and the May 13, 2026 release state the same result.

## State Notes

### Alabama

- **Threshold and who is in it.** Ala. Code 17-13-18(b), as shown in the 2019 enrolled act: "If no candidate receives a majority of all of the votes cast in such primary election for any one office or offices for the nomination to which there were more than two candidates," a second primary is held. 17-13-18(c): "no person can be a candidate except the two persons who receive the highest number of votes."
- **Timing.** Ala. Code 17-13-3(a), as rewritten by 2025 HB258 (enrolled): "a second or runoff primary election shall be held on the fourth Tuesday following the primary election." The same act adds a fourth-Tuesday rule for special primary runoffs (17-13-3(d)).
- **General runoff.** None found in the sections read.
- **Who can vote.** The SOS 2026 Poll Worker Guide, citing 17-13-7.1: a primary voter "may only participate in the primary runoff election of that same party"; a voter who skipped the primary may vote in either party's runoff. Registration for the June 16, 2026 runoff closed June 1 (SOS 2026 Administrative Calendar, citing 17-3-50(a)). The statute text of 17-13-7.1 was not read.
- **Sources.** 2019 HB247 enrolled: https://alison.legislature.state.al.us/files/pdf/SearchableInstruments/2019RS/PrintFiles/HB247-Enr.pdf . 2025 HB258 enrolled: https://alison.legislature.state.al.us/files/pdf/SearchableInstruments/2025RS/HB258-enr.pdf . Read 2026-11-09. The Code of Alabama page (https://alison.legislature.state.al.us/code-of-alabama?section=17-13-18) loads through JavaScript and returned no statute text.
- **Caveats.** HB258's enrolled copy shows passage in both houses on April 24, 2025, and says it takes effect immediately; the governor's signature was not confirmed. The 2019 PDF text shows "sixth fourth Tuesday" because strike-through formatting is lost; the 2025 act confirms "fourth."

### Arkansas

- **Primary runoff.** The State Board of Election Commissioners' 2026 handbook defines elections to include "a general primary election (primary runoff election)" and lists "March 3, 2026 Preferential Primary (Partisan)" and "March 31, 2026 General Primary (Partisan Runoff) Election." That is four weeks.
- **Threshold.** Ark. Code 7-7-304(f), read in the Secretary of State's 2025 code compilation: a candidate with "a majority of the votes cast for that office or position" at the preferential primary is the nominee; otherwise the top two go to the general primary. 7-7-202(b) skips the general primary when no race has three or more candidates (except to break a tie). 7-7-203(a) sets it "four (4) weeks following the preferential primary election."
- **Who can vote.** 7-1-103(a)(18)(B) bars voting in one party's preferential primary and then in "the general primary of another political party."
- **General runoff (local).** Ark. Code 7-5-106(a)(1), as amended by Act 991 of 2025: for "any county elected office or a municipal office," if no candidate "receives a majority of the votes cast," a runoff is held "four (4) weeks following the date of the general election" between the top two. Municipal races also avoid a runoff with 40% plus a 20-point lead (7-5-106(a)(2)). These are local offices, so they are noted but kept out of the state-level count.
- **Other runoffs on the calendar.** The handbook lists "November 3, 2026 ... Nonpartisan General Runoff Election" (nonpartisan judicial and school races first decided in March) and "December 1, 2026 General Runoff and Municipal Runoff Election." Its campaign-finance calendar also lists "Special Runoff Election pursuant to Ark. Code Ann. § 7-5-704 (i.e., general election runoff)."
- **Sources.** Handbook: https://sbec.arkansas.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025-Running-for-Public-Office-9-2-25-FINAL-small-1.pdf . Act 991 of 2025: https://arkleg.state.ar.us/Home/FTPDocument?path=%2FACTS%2F2025R%2FPublic%2FACT991.pdf . Read 2026-11-09.
- **Not a change.** 2025 HB1078, which would have added general election runoffs for state House and Senate, "Died in House Committee at Sine Die adjournment" (https://arkleg.state.ar.us/Bills/Detail?id=HB1078&ddBienniumSession=2025%2F2025R).

### Georgia

- **Threshold and who is in it.** O.C.G.A. 21-2-501(a)(1): no candidate is nominated or elected "unless such candidate shall have received a majority of the votes cast." If no one does, a runoff is held "between the candidates receiving the two highest numbers of votes." This covers primaries, special primaries, elections and special elections.
- **General elections.** 21-2-501(f): "Except for presidential electors, to be elected to public office in a general election, a candidate must receive a majority of the votes cast."
- **Timing.** SB 202 (2021) rewrote the timing rules; the as-passed PDF's plain text mixes struck and new language, so the exact current wording could not be isolated. The state's own 2026 pages show 28 days: georgia.gov lists the "General Primary Runoff Election Day" as "Tuesday, June 16, 2026" and says "If no candidate gets more than 50 percent of the vote in the May Primary, the top two candidates go to a runoff." georgia.gov's runoff page says the runoff is "held 4 weeks after the general election"; its event page lists the general election runoff for December 1, 2026.
- **Who can vote.** 21-2-501(a): only electors entitled to vote in the first primary or election may vote, and "No elector shall vote in a run-off primary or special primary runoff in violation of Code Section 21-2-224." The text of 21-2-224 was not read. The SOS Elections FAQ gives the practical rule: a voter who chose a party ballot in the primary gets the same party's ballot in the runoff, and "If the voter did not vote in the general primary, the voter may choose any party in the Runoff." Municipal runoffs follow 21-2-501(c) (28 days, unless the charter allows plurality).
- **Registration between rounds.** The SOS 2026 calendar gives the June 16 runoff the same registration deadline as the May 19 primary (April 20), with a later "FEDERAL CONTEST REGISTRATION DEADLINE" of May 18. The December 1 general runoff has the same pattern (October 5, or November 2 for federal contests).

SOS 2026 calendar: https://sos.ga.gov/sites/default/files/forms/2026%20Elections%20Calendar_0.pdf . SOS Elections FAQ: https://sos.ga.gov/page/elections-faq (403 to WebFetch, read in Chrome). Read 2026-11-09.

### Louisiana

Louisiana now runs two systems.

- **Open primary system (most offices).** La. R.S. 18:511(A): "A candidate who receives a majority of the votes cast for an office in a primary election is elected." If no one wins, candidates go on to the general election; R.S. 18:482: "the number of candidates for an office who may qualify for the general election is twice the number of persons remaining to be elected to the office" (two for a single seat).
- **Open system dates.** R.S. 18:402(B)(2): gubernatorial years have a "fall primary election held on the third to last Saturday in October" and a "fall general election held on the sixth Saturday after the third to last Saturday in October." R.S. 18:402(C)(2): congressional years have a "fall primary election held on the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November" and a "fall general election held on the sixth Saturday after the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November."
- **Closed party primaries (new).** R.S. 18:2(9): "Party primary office" means an office of senator or representative in the United States Congress, "justice of the supreme court, the State Board of Elementary and Secondary Education, and the Public Service Commission." R.S. 18:410.7(A): "The candidate who receives the majority of votes in the party primary qualifies for the general election" as nominee. If no one does, the top two from each party go to a second party primary (18:410.7(B)).
- **Closed primary dates.** R.S. 18:402(C)(1): in congressional years the spring primary on "the third Saturday in April" serves as the party primary, and the spring general six Saturdays later serves as "if necessary, a second party primary for candidates in a party primary election." The SOS 2026 calendar confirms May 16 ("Party Primary") and June 27 ("2nd Party Primary"), and says 2026 dates were adjusted per Act 640 of 2024 and spring dates per Act 1 of the 2025 First Extraordinary Session. Those acts were not read.
- **U.S. House in 2026.** The SOS calendar says that per Act 7 of the 2026 Regular Session, "U.S. Representative is no longer considered a closed party office" for the November and December cycle; the calendar lists an open U.S. Representative primary on November 3 and general on December 12. Act 7 itself was not read.
- **Who can vote.** R.S. 18:410.3(A): voters vote only for candidates "affiliated with the same party as the voter." 18:410.3(B): unaffiliated voters may vote in one major party primary by choosing that ballot, and "shall not vote in the second party primary of another political party." The SOS closed party primary page says No Party voters "must stay with that party through any necessary runoff." The calendar lists separate registration deadlines for the June 27 second party primary (May 27 in person or by mail, June 6 online).

### Mississippi

- **Constitution, as amended in 2020.** Miss. Const. Art. 5, Sec. 140: "The person receiving a majority of the number of votes cast in the election for these offices shall be declared elected. If no person receives a majority of the votes, then a runoff election shall be held." Section 141 (the old House-district rule) was repealed. Read in the signed resolution HC 47 and in the AG opinion below.
- **General runoff statute.** Miss. Code 23-15-193(2), as amended by SB 2144 (Laws 2024, ch. 513, effective Jan. 1, 2025): if no state officer wins a majority, the top two go "on the ballot for the runoff election to be held four (4) weeks later." The Attorney General's opinion of Nov. 2, 2023 says "the state officers" means only "the statewide elected offices of Governor, Lieutenant Governor, Secretary of State, Attorney General, State Treasurer, Auditor of Public Accounts, Commissioner of Agriculture and Commerce, and Commissioner of Insurance," not legislators or district officers.
- **Primary runoff.** Miss. Code 23-15-191, as amended by SB 2144: "If no candidate receives a majority vote at the election, then the two (2) candidates who receive the highest number of votes" go to a second primary "four (4) weeks later." SB 2144 also changed the congressional primary runoff section (23-15-1031) and others to four weeks. The SOS 2026 County Election Handbook agrees: "Primary Runoff Election four (4) weeks thereafter" (municipal primary runoffs stay at three weeks).
- **Constitution, official copy.** The SOS annotated constitution (updated May 2022) shows the amended Sec. 140 text and Sec. 141 "Repealed."
- **Who can vote.** Mississippi has no party registration. Miss. Code 97-13-35 (quoted in the SOS 2014 presentation) bars voting in one party's primary after voting "on the same date" in another's. The SOS says Attorney General opinions treat the runoff as a continuation of the first primary, so "crossover voting" into another party's runoff "is prohibited under 97-13-35." The same slide deck notes a 2007 federal ruling that "there is no practical way to enforce § 23-15-575." Whether a voter who skipped the first primary may vote in a runoff was not read.

SB 2144 (2024): https://billstatus.ls.state.ms.us/documents/2024/html/SB/2100-2199/SB2144SG.htm ; status "Law," chapter 513, per https://billstatus.ls.state.ms.us/2024/pdf/history/SB/SB2144.xml . AG opinion: https://attorneygenerallynnfitch.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/K.Butler-November-2-2023-Runoff-Election-for-State-Officers.pdf . Read 2026-11-09.

### North Carolina

- **Threshold.** N.C.G.S. 163-111(a)(1): the leader is nominated with a substantial plurality, found "by multiplying the total vote cast for all aspirants by thirty percent (30%)"; "Any excess of the sum so ascertained shall be a substantial plurality."
- **Request.** 163-111(b)(1): no second primary "unless the aspirant receiving the second highest number of votes shall request a second primary." Requests go to the State Board (statewide and multi-county offices) or county board, "no later than 12:00 noon on the ninth day" after the primary (163-111(c)).
- **Who is in it.** "only the two aspirants who received the highest and next highest number of votes shall be voted for."
- **Timing.** 163-111(e): the board "shall order that it be held 10 weeks after the first primary."
- **Who can vote.** 163-111(e): "The second primary is a continuation of the first primary." "There shall be no registration of voters between the dates of the first and second primaries," except people who became qualified in between.
- **Source.** https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_163/GS_163-111.html . Read 2026-11-09.

### Oklahoma

- **Threshold and who is in it.** 26 O.S. 1-103: if no candidate "receives a majority of all votes cast for all candidates of such party for the office," the two highest go to the runoff.
- **Timing.** 26 O.S. 1-103: the runoff is held "the fourth Tuesday of August in the same year." The primary is "on the third Tuesday in June of each even-numbered year" (26 O.S. 1-102).
- **Who can vote.** State Election Board page (last modified May 15, 2026): closed primaries, and "This includes runoff primaries"; "You do not have to participate in a primary election in order to participate in a runoff primary election." No party admitted Independents for 2026-2027.
- **Sources.** https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/DeliverDocument.asp?CiteID=436447 (1-103) and https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/DeliverDocument.asp?CiteID=436446 (1-102). Read 2026-11-09.

### South Carolina

- **Threshold.** S.C. Code 7-17-600: "No candidate shall be declared nominated in a first primary election unless he received a majority of the votes cast." 7-17-610(1): "the majority shall be ascertained by dividing the total vote cast for all candidates by two." Multi-seat races use a per-seat formula (7-17-610(2)).
- **Who is in it and timing.** S.C. Code 7-13-50: "A second primary, when necessary, must be held two weeks after the first," between "the two candidates among those who do not withdraw their candidacies and who received more votes in the first primary." 7-13-40: primaries on "the second Tuesday in June of each general election year," with "a second and third primary each two weeks successively thereafter, if necessary." Both the current and the Jan. 1, 2027 versions of 7-13-40 keep the two-week interval.
- **Who can vote.** The State Election Commission's 2026 primary FAQ: the runoff "is a continuation of the primary," so a primary voter may vote only in the same party's runoff, and someone who voted in neither primary may vote in either party's runoff. 7-13-1040 bars voting in more than one party primary "held the same day."
- **Sources.** https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t07c013.php and https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t07c017.php . https://scvotes.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/2026-04-26-Primary-FAQs.pdf . Read 2026-11-09.

### South Dakota

- **Offices and threshold.** SDCL 12-6-51.1, "Runoff election--United States Senate, House of Representatives, or Governor primary": applies when there are three or more candidates and none "receives thirty-five percent of the votes of the candidate's party."
- **Timing.** "a runoff election shall be held eight weeks from the date of the first primary election."
- **Who is in it.** The top two; if two or more tie for second, "all tying second place candidates must be placed along with the first place candidate on the ballot."
- **Who can vote.** SDCL 12-6-26: only registered members of the party may vote its primary ballot, unless the party opens it to no-party voters. SDCL 12-4-5: voters registered by 5 p.m. 15 days before a runoff are added to the runoff file. 12-6-51.1: the runoff is held "in the same manner as the first election."
- **Source.** https://sdlegislature.gov/api/Statutes/12-6-51.1.html , https://sdlegislature.gov/api/Statutes/12-6-26.html , https://sdlegislature.gov/api/Statutes/12-4-5.html . Read 2026-11-09.

### Texas

- **Threshold.** Tex. Elec. Code 172.003: "a candidate in a primary election must receive a majority of the total number of votes received by all the candidates for the nomination." 172.004(a): if no one does, "a runoff primary election shall be held."
- **Who is in it.** 2.023(a): "the candidates who receive the highest and second highest number of votes."
- **Timing.** 41.007(a): "The general primary election date is the first Tuesday in March in each even-numbered year." 41.007(b): "The runoff primary election date is the fourth Tuesday in May following the general primary election."
- **Who can vote.** 162.013: "A vote in a primary election is void if the voter previously voted in a primary election of another party," or took part in another party's convention that voting year. 162.014 makes it an offense to do so knowingly. A runoff counts as a "primary election" (1.005(14): an election held under Chapter 172; runoffs are under 172.004), and 162.004 refers to voters "in a runoff primary." The SOS (Advisory 2022-11; release of May 13, 2026) says primary voters may vote only in that party's runoff and voters who skipped both primaries may choose either. See "Who Can Vote in a Runoff."
- **General elections.** 2.001 sets a plurality rule "[e]xcept as otherwise provided by law." Runoffs under 2.021 apply to "an election requiring a majority vote," held on a Saturday 30 to 45 days later (2.025(a)), with special congressional and legislative vacancy runoffs 70 to 77 days after the canvass (2.025(d)). No runoff for November state and federal offices was found.

## NCSL Disagreements

1. **State count.** NCSL says "Seven states require a candidate to win a primary with a majority" but lists nine; North Carolina and South Dakota do not use a majority rule.
2. **Texas timing.** NCSL: "normally held six weeks after the primary." Statute: first Tuesday in March to the fourth Tuesday in May (about 12 weeks).
3. **Arkansas timing.** NCSL: "normally held three weeks after the primary." State election board 2026 calendar: March 3 primary, March 31 runoff (four weeks).
4. **Louisiana general election date.** NCSL: "the fifth Saturday after the third-to-last Saturday in October." R.S. 18:402: the "sixth Saturday" after the fall primary, and in congressional years the fall primary is the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November.
5. **Louisiana threshold wording.** NCSL: "If a candidate receives 50% of the vote, they win outright." Statute requires "a majority," which exactly 50% is not.
6. **Louisiana closed party primaries missing.** NCSL lists no Louisiana primary runoff. R.S. 18:410.7 now provides a second party primary for Congress, Supreme Court, BESE and PSC. (Partly a timing issue: NCSL's page predates the 2026 start.)
7. **Arkansas general runoffs missing.** NCSL lists only Georgia, Louisiana and Mississippi. Arkansas law and its 2026 calendar include general runoffs (local offices, nonpartisan judicial runoffs, and 7-5-704 special runoffs).
8. **North Carolina wording.** NCSL: "30% of the vote plus one." Statute: "Any excess" over 30% of the total vote, not exactly one vote. NCSL also lists North Carolina and South Dakota twice.
9. **South Dakota detail.** NCSL says "35% of the votes"; statute says 35% "of the votes of the candidate's party." NCSL gives no timing; statute sets eight weeks.
10. **Mississippi general runoff.** NCSL's "statewide office" matches the AG opinion. NCSL gives no timing; statute now sets four weeks (changed from three in 2024).
