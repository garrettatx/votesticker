# Research: Runoff Elections

## Method

All sources were read on 2026-11-09 with WebFetch, plus direct PDF downloads of official bill and handbook files. The source of record for each row is a state statute on an official legislature site, an enrolled act or bill text published by the legislature, a state attorney general opinion, or a page or document from the state election office. NCSL's "Runoffs in Primary and General Elections" page (updated January 22, 2025), Ballotpedia, news stories, law-publisher mirrors (Justia, FindLaw, Counsel Stack and similar) and search-result summaries were used only as leads to find the official text. Where only a lead supports a fact, it is listed under Not Confirmed and is not in the table as fact. Quotes are kept under 25 words. Some official statute sites (Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Mississippi) serve the code only through a JavaScript app or a LexisNexis portal that could not be fetched, so for those states the text comes from the enrolled act that last amended the section; the notes say so. A third check on Nov 9 (see "Third Check") added state election office documents for Arkansas, Georgia, Louisiana and Mississippi, and the per-state rules on who can vote in a runoff.

### What It Means

- **Skipped the first round:** confirmed yes in Arkansas, Mississippi and North Carolina (unaffiliated voters), joining
  Alabama, Georgia, Oklahoma, South Carolina and Texas. South Dakota is still not stated on an official page.
- **Registering between rounds:** in Arkansas, Mississippi and South Carolina the runoff's registration deadline falls before
  the first election, so a new registration after the first election is too late. In Mississippi and Arkansas the runoff has its
  own deadline (30 days before the runoff), which falls about a day before the first primary, so someone who registers in that
  window can vote in the runoff but not the first primary. South Carolina keeps the first primary's deadline. Oklahoma (about
  ten weeks between rounds, 25-day deadline) and South Dakota (15 days before) allow registering between rounds. North Carolina
  does not, except for people who newly qualify.

Code 1.005(14), 162.003, 162.012, 162.013, 13.143; SOS Advisory 2022-11; SOS release May 13, 2026 |

**How the Texas sections apply to runoffs.** Voting in a party's primary affiliates you with that party (162.003). Once
affiliated, you cannot affiliate with another party that voting year (162.012). A runoff is a "primary election" because it
is held under Chapter 172 (1.005(14); 172.004), so a vote in another party's runoff is void under 162.013 and knowingly
casting one is an offense under 162.014. Someone who voted in no primary has no affiliation yet, so they can pick either
runoff; voting in it affiliates them. The SOS advisory and the May 13, 2026 release state the same result.

### Georgia

### Louisiana

Louisiana now runs two systems.

- **Open primary system (most offices).** La. R.S. 18:511(A): "A candidate who receives a majority of the votes cast for an office in a primary election is elected." If no one wins, candidates go on to the general election; R.S. 18:482: "the number of candidates for an office who may qualify for the general election is twice the number of persons remaining to be elected to the office" (two for a single seat).
- **Open system dates.** R.S. 18:402(B)(2): gubernatorial years have a "fall primary election held on the third to last Saturday in October" and a "fall general election held on the sixth Saturday after the third to last Saturday in October." R.S. 18:402(C)(2): congressional years have a "fall primary election held on the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November" and a "fall general election held on the sixth Saturday after the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November."

- **Closed primary dates.** R.S. 18:402(C)(1): in congressional years the spring primary on "the third Saturday in April" serves as the party primary, and the spring general six Saturdays later serves as "if necessary, a second party primary for candidates in a party primary election." The SOS 2026 calendar confirms May 16 ("Party Primary") and June 27 ("2nd Party Primary"), and says 2026 dates were adjusted per Act 640 of 2024 and spring dates per Act 1 of the 2025 First Extraordinary Session. Those acts were not read.
- **U.S. House in 2026.** The SOS calendar says that per Act 7 of the 2026 Regular Session, "U.S. Representative is no longer considered a closed party office" for the November and December cycle; the calendar lists an open U.S. Representative primary on November 3 and general on December 12. Act 7 itself was not read.
- **Who can vote.** R.S. 18:410.3(A): voters vote only for candidates "affiliated with the same party as the voter." 18:410.3(B): unaffiliated voters may vote in one major party primary by choosing that ballot, and "shall not vote in the second party primary of another political party." The SOS closed party primary page says No Party voters "must stay with that party through any necessary runoff." The calendar lists separate registration deadlines for the June 27 second party primary (May 27 in person or by mail, June 6 online).

### Mississippi

### North Carolina
